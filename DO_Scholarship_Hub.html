What is a D.O., why it perfectly fits your mission, and the exact roadmap to medical school from high school.

Phase 1 • High School (Now)

Clinical Certification & In-State Prep

Graduate from GBHS (Class of 2027) with your CCMA (Certified Clinical Medical Assistant) certification and Foundations of Healthcare training. This puts you years ahead of typical pre-meds with real clinical experience.

Badge: CCMA Clinical Ready
Phase 2 • Undergrad (Years 1–4)

In-State Maryland Pre-Med Major

Enroll in an in-state Maryland university with 100% tuition coverage via MHEC/Meyerhoff. Major in Biology, Biochemistry, or Public Health while working part-time as a certified CCMA in local hospitals for direct patient hours.

Top Picks: UMBC Meyerhoff, UMD College Park, Morgan State, Towson
Phase 3 • Medical School (Years 5–8)

Doctor of Osteopathic Medicine (D.O.)

D.O.s are fully licensed physicians with identical prescribing and surgical privileges to M.D.s, with 200+ extra hours in Osteopathic Manipulative Medicine (OMT)—focusing on the musculoskeletal system, prevention, and compassionate whole-person care.

Regional DO Schools: PCOM (Philly), VCOM (Virginia), Rowan-Virtua (NJ), WVSOM (WV)
Phase 4 • Residency (Years 9–11)

Board-Certified Practicing Physician

Complete medical residency (Pediatrics, OB-GYN, Family Medicine, or Internal Medicine). Return to practice as a culturally competent, trusted physician closing the exact maternal and health disparities you set out to fix.

Outcome: Board-Certified D.O. Physician

✍️ Your Personal Essay: The Winning Narrative

How your essay transforms childhood fear into an undeniable medical mission for scholarship committees.

“The place I spent years trying to avoid became the one place I yearn to be in.”

"The place I spent years trying to avoid became the one place I yearn to be in. Growing up, the pediatrician was always an appointment I wanted to skip past. The smell of antiseptic, the disorienting sounds of a disney movie in the background, and the bright colors bouncing off the walls shook me to my core. This fear evolved to pure hatred. At eleven years old I vowed to never set foot in a doctors office past my eighteenth birthday – a delusional idea – and eventually enter the legal system to fight back against my injustice.

One might ask why I had such a disdain towards the doctors office, but the truth is that I never had a logical reason. It might've been the sheer fact that one person could look at my body and conjure up a diagnosis without my say. Or the fact that I had to face my unhealthy practices embarrassed me to no end. My resentment continued until my sophomore year of high school, where I took my first course in African American Studies. In this class my eyes were opened to numerous wrongs that the Black community has endured in many systems, one being the health care system. I learned that black women – such as myself– had a long history of inequities when it came to their experiences in the medical field. We were statistically treated with less courtesy, respect, and empathy when compared to the average patient. At this moment I realized my fear wasn't displaced, and that there would be a time where I couldn't rely on my own health care providers.

In the same school year I learned about the 'Foundation of health care' program at a school in my county. In which I would be taught about the basics of medicine.
