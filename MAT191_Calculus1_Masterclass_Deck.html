🚗 Real-Life Example: The Tesla Autopilot Speedometer

Why Does Calculus Even Exist?

Imagine driving a Tesla down the highway:

You have two instruments on your dashboard:

  • 1. The Speedometer (The Derivative, $v = ds/dt$): Tells you your exact speed right this millisecond (e.g., $65.4\text{ mph}$). If a deer jumps in front of your car, only your instantaneous speed matters. That is a Derivative.
  • 2. The Odometer / Battery Range (The Integral, $\int v(t) dt$): Adds up every single foot you drove over the past 3 hours to tell you your total distance ($180\text{ miles}$). That is an Integral.

Concrete Sample with Numbers:

Suppose you drove at $60\text{ mph}$ for $1\text{ hour}$, then hit traffic and crawled at $20\text{ mph}$ for $30\text{ minutes}$ ($0.5\text{ hr}$):

$$\text{Total Distance} = (60 \times 1) + (20 \times 0.5) = 60 + 10 = \mathbf{70\text{ miles}}$$

Calculus does this exact multiplication when your speed is constantly curving and changing every second!

UNIT 1 • PRECALC ALGEBRA BOOTCAMP

The 3 Precalc Traps That Destroy Calculus Grades

Before touching limits or derivatives, master these 3 algebra conversions that appear on every single test.

TRAP 1: FRACTIONAL POWERS

Square Roots are Exponents!

Calculus rules do NOT work on radical signs ($\sqrt{}$). Always convert them to powers first:

$$\sqrt{x} = x^{1/2} \quad | \quad \sqrt[3]{x^2} = x^{2/3}$$ $$\frac{1}{\sqrt{x}} = x^{-1/2} \quad | \quad \frac{1}{x^4} = x^{-4}$$
TRAP 2: COMMON FACTORING

Difference of Squares & Cubes

Whenever you see 0/0 in a limit, factoring will save your life:

$$a^2 - b^2 = (a - b)(a + b)$$ $$a^3 - b^3 = (a - b)(a^2 + ab + b^2)$$ $$x^2 - 9 = (x - 3)(x + 3)$$
TRAP 3: LOGARITHM RULES

Expanding Logs Before Differentiating

Expanding logs turns a brutal 10-minute problem into a 5-second problem:

$$\ln(a \cdot b) = \ln(a) + \ln(b)$$ $$\ln(a / b) = \ln(a) - \ln(b)$$ $$\ln(a^k) = k \cdot \ln(a)$$
🔤 Symbol & Conversion Action Decoder:
x^(-n) = 1/x^n: Negative power means it belongs in the denominator.
x^(a/b) = (ᵇ√x)ᵃ: Bottom number is the root, top number is the power.
🔬 Real-Life Example: Microscope Zoom & Earthquake Richter Scales

Why Do Negative Powers Mean Fractions?

Look at a microscope zoom dial or powers of 10 in science:

  • $$10^3 = 1,000$$ (Telescope zoom)
  • $$10^2 = 100$$ (Divide by 10)
  • $$10^1 = 10$$ (Divide by 10)
  • $$10^0 = 1$$ (Divide by 10 - Standard human eyesight!)
  • $$10^{-1} = 0.1 = \frac{1}{10}$$ (Microscope level: Bacteria)
  • $$10^{-2} = 0.01 = \frac{1}{100}$$ (Microscope level: Viruses)
  • $$10^{-6} = 0.000001 = \frac{1}{1,000,000}$$ (Nanometer level: DNA atoms)

The Real-Life Lesson: A negative power is NOT a negative number! It is just a very tiny microscopic fraction ($$10^{-6}$$ is a millionth of a meter). Whenever you see $$\frac{1}{x^3}$$, your brain must instantly say: "Aha! That is just $$x^{-3}$$!"

UNIT 2 • LIMITS & CONTINUITY

Limits: Approaching a Destination (The Bridge Analogy)

A limit doesn't care what happens AT the point; it only cares about where you are WALKING towards!

The One-Sided Limit Rule

For a two-sided limit $\lim_{x \to a} f(x)$ to exist, the left-hand limit must equal the right-hand limit:

$$\lim_{x \to a^-} f(x) = L \quad \text{and} \quad \lim_{x \to a^+} f(x) = L \iff \lim_{x \to a} f(x) = L$$
💡 The Two Hikers on a Bridge
Imagine two hikers walking towards each other on a bridge at $x = 2$.
• If both meet at height $y = 5$, the Limit is 5 (even if there is a hole in the floor!).
• If the bridge broke and one is at height 3 while the other is at height 8, the Limit DOES NOT EXIST (DNE)!

The 3 Types of Discontinuities

  • 1. Removable Discontinuity (Hole): Limit exists, but point $f(c)$ is missing or plotted elsewhere.
  • 2. Jump Discontinuity: Left limit does not equal right limit (piecewise functions).
  • 3. Infinite Discontinuity (Vertical Asymptote): Function shoots to $+\infty$ or $-\infty$.
🔤 Symbol & Direction Decoder:
x → a⁻: Walking from the left (smaller numbers like 1.99).
x → a⁺: Walking from the right (larger numbers like 2.01).
DNE: Does Not Exist (Left and right paths don't meet).
🌉 Real-Life Example: Golden Gate Drawbridge & Potholes

Walking Over a Pothole on the Road

Imagine driving a car down Main Street:

At mile marker $$x = 2$$, construction workers dug a deep pothole ($$y$$ is undefined there). If you drive your car into the hole, you crash ($$0/0$$ divide by zero).

How GPS Navigation calculates the Limit:

Your GPS doesn't look at the hole itself. It looks at where the road is heading from both sides:

  • At mile marker $$x = 1.999\text{ miles}$$, your car elevation is $$3.999\text{ feet}$$.
  • At mile marker $$x = 2.001\text{ miles}$$, your car elevation is $$4.001\text{ feet}$$.

The GPS says: "Even though there is a pothole at marker 2, the road was clearly built to connect at height **4.0 feet**!" That is the Limit! The limit describes the intention of the road, ignoring the missing point!

UNIT 2 • COMPUTATIONAL TECHNIQUES

How to Evaluate Any Limit on an Exam

The battle-tested 4-step decision tree for solving algebraic limits.

TECHNIQUE 1 & 2: PLUG IN OR FACTOR

1. Direct Substitution (Always try first!):

$$\lim_{x \to 3} (x^2 - 4x + 1) = (3)^2 - 4(3) + 1 = \mathbf{-2}$$

2. Factoring (When you get 0/0 indeterminate):

$$\lim_{x \to 2} \frac{x^2 - 4}{x - 2} = \lim_{x \to 2} \frac{(x-2)(x+2)}{x-2} = \lim_{x \to 2} (x+2) = \mathbf{4}$$
TECHNIQUE 3 & 4: CONJUGATES & COMMON DENOMINATORS

3. Conjugate Method (Square roots with 0/0):

$$\lim_{x \to 0} \frac{\sqrt{x+9} - 3}{x} \cdot \frac{\sqrt{x+9} + 3}{\sqrt{x+9} + 3} = \lim_{x \to 0} \frac{(x+9) - 9}{x(\sqrt{x+9}+3)} = \frac{1}{6}$$

4. Common Denominators (Fractions inside fractions):

$$\lim_{x \to 2} \frac{\frac{1}{x} - \frac{1}{2}}{x - 2} = \lim_{x \to 2} \frac{\frac{2 - x}{2x}}{x - 2} = \lim_{x \to 2} \frac{-(x - 2)}{2x(x - 2)} = -\frac{1}{4}$$
🔤 Symbol & Decision Tree:
0/0: Indeterminate Form (Don't panic! It means factor or conjugate!).
Conjugate: Flip the middle sign ($\sqrt{a}-b \to \sqrt{a}+b$).
💊 Real-Life Example: Medicine Dosage in the Bloodstream

Why Do Formulas Give $0/0$ in Real Medicine?

Pharmacologists tracking IV drug injections:

When an IV bag is hooked up to a patient at time $$t = 0$$, the formula for the absorption rate is often written as:

$$\text{Absorption Rate} = \frac{\text{Drug Delivered}}{\text{Time Elapsed}} = \frac{C(t) - C(0)}{t}$$

If you plug in $$t = 0$$ directly, you get $$\frac{0\text{ mg}}{0\text{ seconds}} = \frac{0}{0}$$ (Undefined!).

Does that mean the medicine isn't flowing? No! It just means you asked for the rate over zero seconds. By using factoring or the conjugate method to cancel out the $$t$$ on bottom, you find the initial absorption rate is $$50\text{ mg/min}$$! That is how nurses set automated IV pump flows!

UNIT 2 • INFINITE LIMITS & ASYMPTOTES

Limits at Infinity ($\lim_{x \to \infty}$): The Degree Battle

Finding Horizontal Asymptotes by comparing the highest power of $x$ in the numerator vs. denominator.

CASE 1: DEGREES ARE EQUAL

Top Power = Bottom Power

The answer is simply the ratio of leading coefficients!

$$\lim_{x \to \infty} \frac{5x^3 + 2x - 1}{2x^3 - 7} = \mathbf{\frac{5}{2}}$$

Horizontal Asymptote at $y = 5/2$.

CASE 2: BOTTOM WINS

Bottom Power > Top Power

The denominator grows so fast it pulls the fraction down to zero!

$$\lim_{x \to \infty} \frac{4x^2 + 10}{x^5 - 3x} = \mathbf{0}$$

Horizontal Asymptote at $y = 0$.

CASE 3: TOP WINS

Top Power > Bottom Power

The numerator explodes out of control!

$$\lim_{x \to \infty} \frac{x^4 - 2}{3x^2 + 1} = +\infty$$

No Horizontal Asymptote (DNE).

🔤 Symbol & Asymptote Action:
x → ∞: Looking far into the future (Long-term terminal behavior).
y = L: Horizontal Asymptote (The terminal ceiling line).
🚀 Real-Life Example: Space Rocket Escape Velocity & Parachutes

Terminal Velocity of a Skydiver

When a skydiver jumps out of a plane:

Gravity pulls them downward with speed formula $$v(t) = \frac{180 t}{t + 5}\text{ mph}$$.

  • At $$t = 0\text{ sec}$$, speed is $$0\text{ mph}$$.
  • At $$t = 5\text{ sec}$$, speed is $$\frac{180(5)}{10} = 90\text{ mph}$$.
  • At $$t = 60\text{ sec}$$, speed is $$\frac{180(60)}{65} = 166\text{ mph}$$.
  • As $$t \to \infty$$ (Limit at infinity): $$\lim_{t \to \infty} \frac{180 t}{t + 5} = \frac{180}{1} = \mathbf{180\text{ mph}}$$.

No matter how long the skydiver falls, wind resistance creates a horizontal asymptote at **180 mph** (Terminal Velocity). Calculus finds the terminal speed instantly by comparing the lead powers of $$t$$!

UNIT 2 • CONTINUITY & EXISTENCE THEOREMS

Continuity & The Intermediate Value Theorem (IVT)

How to prove a root exists without solving the equation: The guaranteed exam existence theorem.

The 3-Step Continuity Checklist

A function $f(x)$ is continuous at $x = c$ if and only if:

  1. 1. $f(c)$ is defined (The point actually exists on the graph).
  2. 2. $\lim_{x \to c} f(x)$ exists (Left road connects to right road).
  3. 3. $\lim_{x \to c} f(x) = f(c)$ (The road meets the point with no gap!).

Intermediate Value Theorem (IVT)

If $f(x)$ is continuous on $[a, b]$, it must hit every $y$-value between $f(a)$ and $f(b)$!

📝 Exam Problem: Prove $x^3 - x - 1 = 0$ has a solution on $[1, 2]$.
• $f(1) = (1)^3 - 1 - 1 = \mathbf{-1}\ (< 0)$
• $f(2) = (2)^3 - 2 - 1 = \mathbf{+5}\ (> 0)$
Since $f(x)$ is continuous and goes from negative to positive, by IVT there MUST exist at least one number $c \in (1, 2)$ where $f(c) = 0$!
🔤 Symbol & Theorem Action:
[a, b]: Closed interval (Including the end boundary points).
∃ c ∈ (a, b): "There exists at least one input c inside the interval".
🌡️ Real-Life Example: Airplane Altimeter & Freezing Clouds

Crossing the Freezing Temperature Line

A commercial airplane takes off from Florida:

On the runway ($$0\text{ feet}$$ altitude), the outside air thermometer reads $$+30^\circ\text{C}$$ ($$86^\circ\text{F}$$).

At cruising altitude ($$35,000\text{ feet}$$), the thermometer reads $$-50^\circ\text{C}$$ ($$-58^\circ\text{F}$$).

How pilots use IVT:

Because temperature changes continuously as you fly higher through the atmosphere (it cannot vanish from $$+1^\circ\text{C}$$ and teleport to $$-1^\circ\text{C}$$), by IVT the pilots know with **100% mathematical certainty** that at some altitude between $$0$$ and $$35,000\text{ feet}$$, the temperature was EXACTLY $0^\circ\text{C}$ ($32^\circ\text{F}$)—where ice forms on airplane wings!

UNIT 3 • THE DERIVATIVE FROM FIRST PRINCIPLES

The Limit Definition of the Derivative

The formula that founded modern science: Finding the exact slope of a tangent line at a single point.

The Difference Quotient Formula

$$f'(x) = \frac{df}{dx} = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(x+h) - f(x)}{h}$$
💡 What h Means
$h$ is the distance between your two points. When $h \to 0$, the secant line between two points morphs into the exact **tangent line** kissing the curve at one single point!

Step-by-Step Proof: $f(x) = x^2 \implies f'(x) = 2x$

$$f'(x) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{(x+h)^2 - x^2}{h} = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{x^2 + 2xh + h^2 - x^2}{h}$$ $$= \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{2xh + h^2}{h} = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{h(2x + h)}{h} = \lim_{h \to 0} (2x + h) = \mathbf{2x}$$
🔤 Symbol & Difference Quotient Action:
f'(x): The slope formula (Tells you steepness at any coordinate x).
h: Step size (The distance you step forward before shrinking to 0).
🚔 Real-Life Example: Police Speed Radar Guns

How Police Laser Radar Measures Instant Speed

A police officer shoots a laser at your car:

The laser fires two light pulses separated by a tiny fraction of a second ($$h = 0.001\text{ seconds}$$):

  • Pulse 1 measures your distance: $$x = 500.00\text{ feet}$$.
  • Pulse 2 ($$0.001\text{ sec}$$ later) measures your distance: $$x + h = 500.10\text{ feet}$$.

The radar computer calculates the difference quotient:

$$\text{Speed} = \frac{500.10 - 500.00}{0.001} = \frac{0.10\text{ ft}}{0.001\text{ s}} = \mathbf{100\text{ ft/sec}} = \mathbf{68.2\text{ mph}}$$

Because $$h$$ is practically zero, the radar gun knows your instantaneous velocity at that exact second!

UNIT 3 • SHORTCUT DIFFERENTIATION RULES

The Fundamental Derivative Shortcut Rules

Master these rules and you never have to use the long $h \to 0$ limit formula again!

Rule Name Mathematical Formula High School Rhyme / Memory Trick Worked Example
Power Rule $\frac{d}{dx}[x^n] = n x^{n-1}$ "Bring power to front, subtract 1 from power!" $\frac{d}{dx}[x^7] = 7x^6$
Product Rule $\frac{d}{dx}[f \cdot g] = f'g + fg'$ "First d-second plus second d-first" $\frac{d}{dx}[x^3 \sin x] = 3x^2 \sin x + x^3 \cos x$
Quotient Rule $\frac{d}{dx}\left[\frac{f}{g}\right] = \frac{g f' - f g'}{g^2}$ "Low d-high minus high d-low over square of below!" $\frac{d}{dx}\left[\frac{x^2}{\cos x}\right] = \frac{(\cos x)(2x) - (x^2)(-\sin x)}{\cos^2 x}$
🔤 Symbol & Rule Action:
f·g: Two changing quantities multiplied (e.g., Ticket Price × Customers).
f/g: Changing ratio (e.g., Distance / Time or Mass / Volume).
🎟️ Real-Life Example: Concert Ticket Revenue ($P \times Q$)

Why the Product Rule Needs TWO Terms ($f'g + fg'$)

A music stadium sells concert tickets:

$$\text{Total Revenue} = \text{Price per Ticket } (P) \times \text{Quantity of Fans } (Q)$$

If the stadium increases ticket price ($P$ rises), two things happen at the same time:

  • Term 1 ($P' \cdot Q$): You make more money per fan who buys a ticket.
  • Term 2 ($P \cdot Q'$): But higher prices scare some fans away ($Q$ drops!).

You cannot just multiply their individual changes; you must add both effects together: $$\frac{d(\text{Revenue})}{dt} = P'Q + PQ'$$. That is why the Product Rule has two parts!

UNIT 3 • TRIGONOMETRIC DIFFERENTIATION

Trigonometric Derivatives & The "Co-" Negative Rule

How to memorize all 6 trig derivatives instantly using the "Co-" negative sign pattern.

The Primary 3 Trig Derivatives

$$\frac{d}{dx}[\sin x] = +\cos x$$ $$\frac{d}{dx}[\tan x] = +\sec^2 x$$ $$\frac{d}{dx}[\sec x] = +\sec x \tan x$$

The "Co-" Derivatives (Always Negative!)

$$\frac{d}{dx}[\mathbf{co}s x] = \mathbf{-\sin x}$$ $$\frac{d}{dx}[\mathbf{co}t x] = \mathbf{-\csc^2 x}$$ $$\frac{d}{dx}[\mathbf{co}sec x] = \mathbf{-\csc x \cot x}$$
🔤 Symbol & Pattern Action:
"Co-": If the function starts with "Co", its derivative MUST be negative!
💓 Real-Life Example: Hospital EKG Heart Monitor

Tracking Heart Rhythms with Sine & Cosine

A doctor watches a patient's heartbeat on a monitor:

The electrical voltage in the heart muscle pulses as a wave: $$V(t) = \sin(t)$$.

The doctor needs to know the current flow (rate of voltage change):

$$\text{Current} = \frac{dV}{dt} = \cos(t)$$

  • When voltage is at its baseline ($$\sin(0) = 0$$), the electrical spark is firing at maximum speed ($$\cos(0) = 1$$)!
  • When voltage reaches its highest peak ($$\sin(\pi/2) = 1$$), the spark pauses for a millisecond before reversing ($$\cos(\pi/2) = 0$$)!
UNIT 3 • THE MOST IMPORTANT RULE IN CALCULUS

The Chain Rule: Peeling the Russian Doll

How to differentiate composite functions $f(g(x))$: Derivative of outside × derivative of inside!

The Formula: $\frac{d}{dx}[f(g(x))] = f'(g(x)) \cdot g'(x)$

$$\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{dy}{du} \cdot \frac{du}{dx}$$
💡 The Russian Doll / Onion Analogy
When you peel an onion, you peel the outer shell first while leaving the core untouched, then you multiply by the derivative of what was inside!

Step-by-Step Worked Examples

Example 1: $y = (3x^2 + 5)^4$

$$\frac{dy}{dx} = 4(3x^2 + 5)^3 \cdot \frac{d}{dx}[3x^2 + 5] = 4(3x^2 + 5)^3 \cdot (6x) = \mathbf{24x(3x^2+5)^3}$$
🔤 Symbol & Chain Action:
dy/du · du/dx: Multiplying gear speed multipliers together.
⚙️ Real-Life Example: Sports Car 6-Speed Transmission

Bicycle & Car Transmission Gears

Pressing the gas pedal in a car:

  • Gas pedal position ($x$) increases Engine RPM ($u$) by $$4\times$$: $$\frac{du}{dx} = 4$$.
  • Engine RPM ($u$) turns the wheels ($y$) through the transmission gearbox by $$3\times$$: $$\frac{dy}{du} = 3$$.

How much do your car wheels accelerate when you step on the gas?

$$\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{dy}{du} \cdot \frac{du}{dx} = 3 \times 4 = \mathbf{12\times\text{ faster}}$$!

The Chain Rule simply multiplies mechanical gear ratios together across the entire drivetrain!

UNIT 4 • ADVANCED DIFFERENTIATION

Implicit Differentiation & Tangent Line Equations

Finding slopes on curves where $y$ cannot be isolated (like circles, ellipses, and foliums).

The Golden Rule of $y$: Attach $\frac{dy}{dx}$

Whenever you take the derivative of any term with $y$, multiply by $\frac{dy}{dx}$ because $y$ is secretly a function of $x$ ($y = y(x)$):

$$\frac{d}{dx}[x^3] = 3x^2 \quad \text{vs} \quad \frac{d}{dx}[y^3] = \mathbf{3y^2 \frac{dy}{dx}}$$ $$\frac{d}{dx}[xy] = (1)(y) + (x)\left(\frac{dy}{dx}\right) = \mathbf{y + x\frac{dy}{dx}}$$

Finding the Equation of a Tangent Line

Find the tangent line to $x^2 + y^2 = 25$ at point $(3, 4)$:

$$2x + 2y\frac{dy}{dx} = 0 \implies \frac{dy}{dx} = -\frac{x}{y}$$ $$\text{Plug in } (3, 4) \implies m = -\frac{3}{4}$$ $$y - y_1 = m(x - x_1) \implies \mathbf{y - 4 = -\frac{3}{4}(x - 3)}$$
🔤 Symbol & Tangent Action:
dy/dx: The hidden slope attached to every y-term.
🎡 Real-Life Example: Ferris Wheel Cabin & Satellite Orbit

Satellite Orbit Tangent Release

A satellite orbits Earth in a circle ($x^2 + y^2 = 25$):

If the satellite turns off its thrusters at coordinate $$(3, 4)$$, Newton's First Law says it will fly off in a straight line along the Tangent Line!

Implicit differentiation gives the exact exit slope: $$m = -3/4 = -0.75$$. NASA engineers use this exact tangent line equation to calculate where a space probe will fly into deep space!

UNIT 4 • APPLIED RATES OF CHANGE

Related Rates: The 5-Step Master Blueprint

Conquering the most feared word problems in Calculus I: Sliding ladders, filling cones, and walking shadows.

The 5-Step Problem Solving Recipe

  1. Step 1: Draw a diagram and assign variable letters ($x, y, h, r$).
  2. Step 2: Write down what rates you are GIVEN ($dx/dt$) and NEED ($dy/dt$).
  3. Step 3: Connect variables using geometry ($x^2+y^2=13^2$).
  4. Step 4: Differentiate w.r.t Time $t$ using Chain Rule!
  5. Step 5: Plug in numbers AFTER taking derivatives!

Worked Problem: Sliding Ladder

A 13-ft ladder slides down a wall. If bottom moves away at $2\text{ ft/s}$ ($dx/dt=2$), how fast is the top sliding down ($dy/dt$) when $x=5$?

$$x^2 + y^2 = 13^2 = 169 \implies 2x\frac{dx}{dt} + 2y\frac{dy}{dt} = 0$$ $$\text{When } x=5, y=12 \implies (5)(2) + (12)\frac{dy}{dt} = 0 \implies \mathbf{\frac{dy}{dt} = -0.833\text{ ft/s}}$$
🔤 Symbol & Rate Action:
d/dt: Rate with respect to TIME in seconds or hours.
🚒 Real-Life Example: Firetruck Rescue Ladder on a Building

Why Firefighters Must Know Related Rates

A firetruck extends a 13-foot ladder against a burning apartment:

If the truck wheels roll backwards away from the building at a steady $$2\text{ ft/sec}$$, what happens to the top of the ladder?

  • When the ladder is near the top ($$x=5, y=12$$), the top drops slowly at $$-0.833\text{ ft/s}$$.
  • When the ladder slides near the ground ($$x=12, y=5$$), plug in the math: $$(12)(2) + (5)\frac{dy}{dt} = 0 \implies \frac{dy}{dt} = \mathbf{-4.8\text{ ft/s}}$$!

The Safety Warning: As the ladder flattens, the top crashes down 6× faster! Firefighters use related rates to ensure ladders never collapse unexpectedly!

UNIT 4 • GRAPH BEHAVIOR & ANALYSIS

Curve Sketching: 1st & 2nd Derivative Tests

How derivatives tell you the exact shape, peaks, valleys, and bends of any graph.

1st Derivative ($f'(x)$): Slope & Peaks/Valleys

  • $f'(x) > 0$: Function is INCREASING ($\nearrow$).
  • $f'(x) < 0$: Function is DECREASING ($\searrow$).
  • $f'(x) = 0$: Critical Point (Candidate for max/min).
  • First Derivative Test: $+ \to -$ is Local Max; $- \to +$ is Local Min.

2nd Derivative ($f''(x)$): Concavity (Curvature)

  • $f''(x) > 0$: Concave UP ($\smile$ "Holds water").
  • $f''(x) < 0$: Concave DOWN ($\frown$ "Spills water").
  • Inflection Point: Where curvature flips ($\smile \leftrightarrow \frown$).
🔤 Symbol & Curve Action:
f'(x) = 0: Flat peak or valley.
f''(x) = 0: Inflection point where steering wheel straightens.
🎢 Real-Life Example: Cedar Point Roller Coaster Engineering

Designing Roller Coaster Loops & Drops

Roller coaster engineers at theme parks use concavity:

  • Top of the Lift Hill: $$f'(x) = 0$$ and $$f''(x) < 0$$ (Concave Down). Riders experience weightlessness (0 G's).
  • The Inflection Point: Halfway down the drop ($$f''(x) = 0$$), the track transitions from bending down to curving up. This is where acceleration shifts smoothest so riders don't get whiplash.
  • Bottom of the Valley: $$f'(x) = 0$$ and $$f''(x) > 0$$ (Concave Up). Maximum positive G-force pushing riders into their seats!
UNIT 4 • REAL-WORLD OPTIMIZATION

Applied Optimization: Finding the Perfect Solution

Finding maximum profit, minimum building cost, and optimal nuclear harvest times.

The 4-Step Optimization Blueprint

  1. 1. Objective Function: Write what to maximize/minimize ($A = x \cdot y$).
  2. 2. Constraint Equation: Use given limits ($2x + y = 100 \implies y = 100 - 2x$).
  3. 3. Combine into Single Variable: $A(x) = x(100 - 2x) = 100x - 2x^2$.
  4. 4. Set Derivative to ZERO: $A'(x) = 100 - 4x = 0 \implies \mathbf{x = 25\text{ m},\ y = 50\text{ m}}$.

Worked Problem: Maximum Area Pasture

$$\text{Max Area} = 25 \times 50 = \mathbf{1,250\text{ m}^2}$$
🔤 Symbol & Optimization Action:
Objective Function: The goal you want to maximize or minimize.
Constraint: The budget or physical resource limit.
📦 Real-Life Example: Amazon Cardboard Box Packaging

Minimizing Cardboard to Save Millions of Dollars

Amazon ships 5 billion packages every year:

If a box must hold $$1,000\text{ cm}^3$$ of volume ($$x^2 y = 1000$$), what box dimensions use the absolute least cardboard (Surface Area)?

$$\text{Surface Area} = x^2 + 4xy = x^2 + \frac{4000}{x}$$

Take derivative and set to zero: $$2x - \frac{4000}{x^2} = 0 \implies 2x^3 = 4000 \implies x = \mathbf{12.6\text{ cm}}$$ (a perfect cube!).

By optimizing box dimensions with calculus, Amazon saved over **$50,000,000 in paper costs** last year alone!

UNIT 5 • INTEGRATION & THE FTC

Integrals & The Fundamental Theorem of Calculus

The grand climax of Calculus: Integration is the exact inverse of Differentiation!

Riemann Sums: Adding Infinite Rectangles

$$\int_a^b f(x)dx = \lim_{n \to \infty} \sum_{i=1}^n f(x_i^*) \Delta x = \text{Exact Area Under Curve}$$

The Fundamental Theorem of Calculus (FTC)

$$\text{Part 1: } \frac{d}{dx}\left[ \int_a^x f(t)dt \right] = f(x)$$ $$\text{Part 2: } \int_a^b f(x)dx = F(b) - F(a) \quad (\text{where } F'(x) = f(x))$$

Example: $\int_1^4 (3x^2 + 2) dx = \left[ x^3 + 2x \right]_1^4 = 72 - 3 = \mathbf{69}$

🔤 Symbol & Integral Action:
F(b) - F(a): Antiderivative at top boundary minus bottom boundary.
dx: Width of each infinitesimal slice.
⚡ Real-Life Example: Electric Car Battery Solar Charging

Calculating Total Kilowatt-Hours from Changing Solar Panels

A solar roof charges an Electric Car:

Because clouds pass by and the sun moves across the sky, solar power output ($P(t)$ in kW) fluctuates constantly: $$P(t) = 3 \sin(\pi t / 12)\text{ kW}$$.

How much total energy (kWh) is in the battery after 12 hours?

$$\text{Total Energy} = \int_0^{12} 3 \sin\left(\frac{\pi t}{12}\right) dt = \left[ -3 \cdot \frac{12}{\pi} \cos\left(\frac{\pi t}{12}\right) \right]_0^{12}$$

$$= \left( \frac{36}{\pi} - \left( -\frac{36}{\pi} \right) \right) = \frac{72}{\pi} \approx \mathbf{22.9\text{ kWh}} = \mathbf{90\text{ miles of driving}}$$!

The FTC allows solar engineers to compute total battery charge without measuring every single electron!

UNIT 5 • INTEGRATION TECHNIQUES

Integration by Substitution ($u$-Substitution)

The reverse of the Chain Rule: Changing variables to make ugly integrals trivial.

The 4-Step $u$-Sub Recipe

  1. Step 1: Pick $u = g(x)$ (the inside piece).
  2. Step 2: $du = g'(x)dx \implies dx = \frac{du}{g'(x)}$.
  3. Step 3: Substitute into integral and cancel out all $x$'s!
  4. Step 4: Integrate w.r.t $u$, then substitute back $x$ (+C)!

Worked Exam Example: $\int 2x e^{x^2} dx$

$$\text{Set } u = x^2 \implies du = 2x dx \implies dx = \frac{du}{2x}$$ $$\int 2x e^u \frac{du}{2x} = \int e^u du = e^u + C = \mathbf{e^{x^2} + C}$$
🔤 Symbol & Substitution Action:
u: The replacement variable that simplifies the messy core.
+ C: Constant of Integration (Don't forget +C on indefinite integrals!).
